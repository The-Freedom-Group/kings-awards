03 — How I Build

Five decisions, and what they cost.

decide.

The same shape every time: a constraint, a decision, what was built, the result, and what it changed.

01Rethinking a traditional category
Constraint
Fire safety was sold by distributors to engineers, on account, with catalogues. Nobody was selling it well to a person with a phone and a problem.
Decision
Treat the category like any other online essential: own the product, explain it plainly, ship it next day, and build the tools that answer the question before the customer has to ask.
Implementation
A business store built over 12 months, direct manufacturing and the Firestorm brand in 2024, and buying that replaces a phone call: clear specifications, kits and tracked delivery.
Result
Number one of 1,193 fire extinguisher sellers on eBay UK by July 2026, and a category that is now bought the way any other online essential is.
02Turning traction into influence
Constraint
A young company had no standing with manufacturers, marketplaces or the trade.
Decision
Let the numbers speak first, then use every invitation to tell the story of how they were made.
Implementation
One of five finalists, from hundreds of entrants, in the eBay UK Business Awards, Social Impact, 2024, with an invitation to Belfast to meet eBay's UK leadership. Shortlisted for the Manchester Young Talent Awards' Agent of Change award in 2026. Approached by Temu UK to open its fire extinguisher category. A global producer that chose a small Greater Manchester warehouse as its first UK client. Entered for the King's Awards for Enterprise 2027.
Result
Growth over two comparable years, recognition, and a seat in the conversations that decide how the category is sold.
03Designing the operating system
Constraint
Every new channel and brand multiplied the work. Done by hand, the founder becomes the bottleneck.
Decision
Build one set of shared infrastructure that every product, channel and person plugs into, then hire and train people to run it.
Implementation
Direct sourcing with an in-house supply-chain tracker. One warehouse serving every channel. Order management and a shared support desk. Listing, pricing and compliance workflows built once for eBay and reused for Temu, and now being reused for Amazon, OnBuy and Shopify. Brand and IP held in the company's name.
Result
Everything built once for eBay was reused for Temu, then for Amazon, OnBuy and Shopify. Three more channels were prepared in 2026 without a second warehouse or a second team.
04Adapting under pressure
Constraint
Temu had become a large part of the business, and containers of stock had been ordered just for that platform. Then Temu's compliance system took the whole store offline overnight, more than once, while the company was working with Temu's UK team on its rules. At the same time, disruption in the Red Sea pushed freight costs up and made container planning harder.
Decision
No single marketplace, supplier or product would be allowed to decide the company's future.
Implementation
Compliance files were strengthened and relationships built with platform leaders and manufacturers. The Amazon launch and the own website were brought forward. The range widened into alarms, batteries, gloves and cleaning products. Forecasting, stock control and cash-flow planning were tightened.
Result
Disruption became the strategy: a multi-brand, multi-channel group, rather than one store on one platform.
05Potential, not polished CVs
Constraint
A growing company needs people, and the usual route, a polished CV and a standard interview, misses a lot of potential.
Decision
Hire on potential, not pedigree. Recruit through college placements, Jobcentre Plus and word of mouth, and give people real responsibility early.
Implementation
Placements hosted since 2021 with New Bridge College, T Level placements with Bury College from October 2025, Jobcentre Plus referrals, and Disability Confident Committed status. Adjustments are offered before the interview, and every placement starts with a live brief the person owns.
Result
Roles in web development, warehouse and fulfilment, IT, ecommerce and servicing filled by people who came in this way. All three T Level students in the first cohort moved into paid work in 2026. Two of them built the company's website and storefronts from scratch.
Find a non-seasonal niche: a product everybody needs and nobody wants, where the competition is less. Then make this boring product sexy. Build a system around it, and repeat.
Tom Letcher, Founder

Carbon footprint

Longer life. Fewer miles. Less waste.

Where the footprint sits, and what is done about each part.

  • Refurbishbefore replace
  • Full containersnot part loads
  • Specialist recoveryat end of life

Longer life

Inspection, servicing and compliant refurbishment extend service life instead of replacing fire extinguishers before they need it. The range is moving with the industry away from fluorinated foam.

End of life

Expired, discharged or damaged fire extinguishers go to specialist partners, CheckFire Group and Jewel Fire Group, for controlled processing and metal recovery. Waste-transfer documentation gives traceability.

Freight

Purchasing is consolidated into full sea-freight containers, planned by forecasting: fewer fragmented consignments, less emergency freight, less over-ordering and obsolete stock.

Packaging and waste

Recyclable cardboard and right-sized protection. Incoming cartons, pallets and protective materials are reused where suitable; cardboard, plastic and scrap metal are separated in the warehouse. Digital records replace routine printing.

Buying decisions

Durability, packaging volume, freight efficiency, return rates and end-of-life requirements are weighed alongside safety and compliance whenever suppliers and products are reviewed.

Next

A formal carbon and energy baseline, recorded collection volumes and the weight of fire extinguishers recovered, measured packaging use, reduction targets, and a customer take-back route.

Tom's biography

Before
Freedom

Before there was Freedom Fire, there was a schoolboy with a bag full of Lucozade, KitKat Chunkies and an instinct for spotting opportunity.

My dad loaned me the money to buy that first batch of chocolate and drinks. It was the first business loan I ever had, arranged without paperwork, credit checks or interest. It gave me my first lesson in business: somebody may help you begin, but what happens next is your responsibility.

From August 2005, throughout childhood and into university, I kept starting small ventures: selling at school, cleaning neighbours' cars for money, car boot sales growing up, and side hustles alongside my studies. Each did well in its own respect, and every one taught me something about customers, margins, risk and resilience. Business was never something I discovered later in life. The instinct had always been there.

Life beyond those early ventures was turbulent. I attended St. Monica's High School. In March 2012, when I was 15, I lost my mum, Michele, to breast cancer. I left school that July and carried the loss into adulthood, and in September 2014 into the University of Salford, where I began studying architecture.

Architecture taught me far more than how to design buildings. It trained me to respect detail, understand how systems connect and transform an empty space into a workable vision. It taught me research, presentation, problem solving and the discipline of revising an idea repeatedly until every element served a purpose.

In July 2015, at 19, my best friend died in a motorbike accident. Grief derailed my second year and I came close to losing my place at university. But I recovered, graduated in July 2017 and continued directly into a two-year Master's.

During my Master's, I explored how design could create opportunity for young people who felt disconnected from education and society. In July 2019, the month I graduated from the Master's, that journey took me to a summer placement at Harvard University and MIT, an experience that felt almost impossible considering how close I had come to leaving university altogether.

In August 2019, aged 23, I entered the Manchester Evening News and Salboy competition to win a £150,000 apartment. Entrants had to propose a creative scheme that would benefit Salford's surrounding community. My proposal continued the work behind my Master's thesis: helping young men like me move away from the streets and towards education, purpose and opportunity.

I reached the final and finished as runner-up.

At the time, it felt like the greatest professional loss of my young life. I believed winning could provide security and perhaps the platform from which everything else would begin. Losing taught me something more valuable: no competition, employer or institution was going to discover my ambition for me. Nobody else could find the hunger inside me, and nobody was coming to hand me my future.

Architecture had not rejected me. In October 2019 I was offered two architecture jobs and turned them both down. There was something inside me that knew I could not spend my life working for somebody else. Ironically, the only boss I ever had was my dad.

Instead, I went to work for his cleaning company as a delivery driver. On paper, it looked like a dramatic step backwards: from architecture, a Master's degree and Harvard to loading cleaning products into a van. There was no impressive title, carefully designed studio or obvious career path. But sometimes the route towards your future looks nothing like progress while you are travelling through it.

In December 2019, during one ordinary delivery, I came across a second-hand fire extinguisher.

To anybody else, it was an old piece of equipment.
To me, it looked like the beginning of something.

From that point I built Freedom Fire as a sole trader, and on 27 August 2021 it became a limited company. Over the years that followed we went on to establish rapid ecommerce growth, becoming number one for fire extinguishers on eBay and number one on Temu.

We endured more loss along the way: another close friend when I was 27, and my sister-in-law when I was 28. Through the hardship, even when the chips were down, we carried on, and became the start of what we like to call a £100 million enterprise.
